# Forecasting subway entries and nearby bike departures after service alerts

Working results section, 28 November 2026

## Data and forecast task

We study whether station-specific instructions in a public subway disruption notice help predict travel counts during the next complete local clock hour. The two outcomes are subway entries at a station complex and bike departures from docks assigned to that complex. A notice can name one station as a temporary terminus and another as a transfer point. Testing the contribution of that distinction requires a comparison that retains the same station identities, histories, forecast origins and outcome masks.

In the February 2026 New York City development cohort, we recovered 1,752 original service notices from the MTA archive. We defined candidate complexes from the affected routes in the base timetable available before the notices. We counted a complex-hour once, assigning overlapping events to the earliest notice. This yielded 67,824 unique complex-hours, of which 44,938 had observations for both modes. The cohort includes delays, maintenance and storm-related changes. The effect of unscheduled disruptions requires a narrower, validated eligibility rule. [Development cohort, methods and source hashes](../research/alert_study/february/README.md)

## Development results

A seasonal forecast repeated each complex's count from the same hour the previous week. On 32,903 matched complex-hours during February 8–28, its mean absolute error was 93.10 subway entries and 3.61 bike departures per complex-hour. Across three successive target weeks, subway MAE ranged from 61.82 to 114.13, and bike MAE ranged from 2.70 to 5.08. This comparison assumes access to operational counts before the public monthly release. [Weekly scores and denominators](../research/alert_study/february/weekly_naive.csv)

An internal review of 40 text-screened notices and 80 sampled screen-negative notices documented 31 cases with explicit, different instructions for two named stations. Both stations appeared on the initial affected-route map in 29 cases. Only 14 cases retained both stations and both modal targets after overlap and missingness checks. These 14 cases spanned seven distinct station pairs on ten dates. The retrospective effective timetable agreed with the base feed on pair membership for all 31 reviewed positives. Two confirmed cases came from the sampled screen negatives, motivating broader coverage of alternative-service language. [Role evidence](../research/alert_study/february/review.csv) · [Timetable cross-check](../research/alert_study/february/effective_schedule.csv)

The prevalence of usable role comparisons in the full population remains uncertain because most first notices lacked a manual text review. No language model was evaluated on forecast error in this campaign. A chronological comparison against a strong text-aware baseline, paired uncertainty across time blocks, and an independent-city replication remain necessary to support the proposed predictive claim.

## Chronological February pilot

Two expanding-window fits used only earlier completed target hours and purged training examples from recurring notices that appeared in the evaluation period. Across 19,782 alert-exposed station-hours during February 15–28, the weekly seasonal forecast had the lowest subway MAE (113.73 entries), and the history-and-calendar ridge model had the lowest bike MAE (3.94 departures) among the tested models. We then compared two otherwise identical ridge models on seven incidents for which both named stations had both outcomes and both weekly lags. Giving the model each reviewed station's role changed subway MAE from 145.25 to 144.42 entries and bike MAE from 5.46 to 5.53 departures. Exchanging the roles within each notice gave 140.92 entries and 5.70 departures. Omitting the single February 16 incident reversed the direction of the subway comparison. Only four dates contributed these paired results. More independently labelled incidents and later validation periods are needed to estimate the value of station-specific language. [Forecast design, results and row-level predictions](../research/alert_study/february_pilot/README.md)

## First-notice wording and automated role extraction

Across January–April, explicit wording rules assigned at least one station role in 877 of 7,273 first notices. In the February development annotations, the rules recovered 39 of 62 reviewed station-role labels. A targeted internal reading of 12 March and April notices missed by a strict different-role screen found ten with distinguishable station instructions. Four repeated the same local-service and lower-level boarding wording. The limited coverage and recurrence of these constructions constrain estimates of the number of independent comparisons.

We added full initial-notice TF-IDF features to the February numerical model, fitting the vocabulary on training incidents within each fold. On 103 rule-named station-hours from 63 later incidents across 12 dates, adding local station identities to the text model reduced MAE from 202.02 to 187.44 subway entries and from 5.013 to 4.872 bike departures. Adding explicit role labels to the same inputs yielded 192.97 entries and 4.947 departures. The seasonal rail forecast had lower error on those same rows (161.52 entries). Only one scored incident retained two stations with disjoint extracted role sets. More complete entity resolution and a larger number of within-incident comparisons are needed before evaluating a language-model advantage. [Extraction audit, forecast comparisons and source spans](../research/alert_study/role_screen/README.md)

## Four-month support and April forecasts

The January–April panel joined 7,273 original notices and their archived versions to 282,280 distinct station-hours. Of these, 194,871 had measured entries and nearby bike departures and 190,647 also had both weekly lags. We fixed each monthly dock catchment using the previous month’s final seven days and used base transit timetables available before the applicable service dates. The text rules identified 34 incidents with two stations whose extracted role sets were disjoint. Twenty-four retained such a pair after timetable mapping and earliest-notice assignment, and 19 retained both outcomes and lags. Those 19 spanned 18 dates across 11 weeks. Each retained a pair with scheduled revenue calls in a separate retrospective timetable check. Allowing unequal role sets that shared a label increased the final count to 31.

We fitted the fixed ridge models on 142,669 January–March station-hours and evaluated 47,978 April station-hours. The previous week’s same-hour count had the lowest network MAE in both modes among the seasonal and ridge forecasts (80.88 entries and 7.454 departures). On 219 station-hours with a rule-derived local instruction, that baseline yielded 127.55 entries and 9.868 departures in MAE. The full text and station identity model yielded 215.75 and 12.676. Adding the local roles yielded 213.90 and 12.700. Only five April incidents retained fully observed stations with disjoint roles. The April text and cases had informed parser development. These comparisons support retaining the weekly baseline for later validation and leave the incremental value of an independently assessed language model unresolved. [Dated panel, denominators and paired forecasts](../research/alert_study/winter_spring/README.md)

After inspecting the ridge results, we fitted an exploratory boosted tree to the deviation from the previous week’s count. With full first-notice text features, network MAE was 80.96 subway entries and 7.388 bike departures. Against weekly seasonal, this corresponds to a 0.10% increase in subway error and a 0.88% reduction in bike error. The equal-mode reduction was 0.39% (descriptive 95% interval −3.19% to +4.18% under paired seven-day block resampling). Adding explicit station-mention and role indicators produced identical April predictions at saved precision. The anchored tree provides a stronger baseline than the ridge models on this exposed period. A language-specific forecast claim requires an extraction audit and later validation against both numerical and text-aware controls. [Boosted comparisons and uncertainty](../research/alert_study/winter_spring/boost_blocks.csv)

## Station-role extraction before May demand evaluation

We compared the rule parser and a fixed, four-bit Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct model on first notices without displaying travel outcomes. The initial stratified sample contained 28 notices and 42 station–role assignments from an internal text-only reference. Rules correctly recovered 30 assignments and produced no extras. The zero-shot model returned an empty array for 27 notices and recovered one assignment. In nine additional notices selected for rarer wording, the rules recovered 17 of 38 assignments. Qwen recovered eight and produced one extra. These deliberately enriched samples do not estimate role prevalence in the archive.

After inspecting those results, we fixed an example-based prompt and a separate 20-notice sample before follow-up inference. On the same 23 reference assignments, the initial prompt recovered four with six extras, the example prompt recovered 18 with 23 extras, and the rules recovered ten with zero extras. Several incorrect assignments were grounded in real station names but attached an unsupported operational role, including a brake-investigation location classified as skipped. On the 22 assignments with an identifiable station complex, the example prompt correctly recovered 17 and rules recovered eight. One internal reference, recurring wording and few examples of some roles limit generalization. The forecast comparison was registered before access to May demand and will test whether increased extraction coverage contributes predictive information beyond the fixed weekly, boosted text and rule controls. ## May validation population

We applied the registered timetable-availability filter to 1,777 original May notices. The 1,743 eligible notices yielded 66,123 distinct alert-exposed station-hours before the outcome mask. Both target counts were observed for 46,842 station-hours. Both weekly lags were also observed for 46,621 station-hours from 1,593 incidents on 31 dates. Removing notices linked to the text audit by ID, exact input or recurrence group left 44,860 station-hours from 1,534 incidents. A separately retained group of 34 notices preceded verified availability of the succeeding timetable.

Rules assigned a local station role on 242 paired rows from 151 incidents. Only one incident retained two stations with disjoint extracted roles. On May 27, a 15:03 notice reported an A-train suspension and turnbacks. A 15:15 revision announced delays after removal of a train. In the 16:00 target hour, Inwood–207 St, 145 St and 168 St recorded 358, 904 and 2,114 subway entries, respectively. Their assigned docks recorded seven, 26 and 21 departures. We verified all six counts from fare-category records and individual trip starts. The revision illustrates that the service described at issue can change before the forecast hour. The number of comparable station pairs recovered by the language model and their forecast errors remain open. [Validation panel, source records and forecast files](../research/alert_study/may_validation/panel/README.md)

### How many notices can distinguish two station roles?

Across the 46,621 paired May station-hours, 1,254 (2.69%) had a station name in the original message that resolved to the assigned complex. Of the 1,593 notices, 597 mentioned no retained station and 830 mentioned one. This leaves at most 166 notices (10.4%) for the within-notice role exchange, or 137 after excluding parser-audited notices and linked cases. The full set of 166 spans all 31 dates. The exchange requires different role assignments at two stations, so realized support may be smaller. We counted all resolvable mentions, including background references in planned-work reminders. [Calculation and per-notice inventory](../research/alert_study/may_validation/name_support/README.md)

A single internal AI-assisted reader reviewed 32 of the 166 name-eligible notices selected by a fixed hash ordering. Three notices gave two retained stations disjoint roles, 20 assigned overlapping roles, and nine gave at most one retained station a current role. Five of the 32 were linked to previous parser audits, including two of the three qualifying cases. A version-history check after annotation found that all three qualifying notices had been revised to report at least partial restoration before the forecast hour. On May 23, for example, the initial notice identified express boundaries at 125 St and Grand Central-42 St and lower-level boarding at 86 St. Four minutes later a revision announced local service and upper-level boarding, forty minutes before the forecast hour. For the eight station-hours in these three cases, source reaggregation reproduced every subway and bike target and its corresponding weekly input. The target counts came from 55 fare-category rows and 123 distinct bike starts across 51 mapped docks. These examples motivate reporting how often distinct instructions remain current at the target hour. They do not estimate forecast accuracy. [Reading protocol, annotations and eight linked station-hours](../research/alert_study/may_validation/name_support/README.md#how-many-mentions-describe-different-instructions)

### Availability of public inputs at issue time

Only 801 of the 46,621 paired May station-hours (1.72%), from 34 first notices on May 6, passed a necessary public-source timing check. At issue, the trip objects used for both the dock map and weekly bike count had to predate the notice, a source row for each station location had to exist, and all current fare rows contributing to the weekly rail count had to exist. At least one contributing fare row in every matched weekly total was updated after issue. The data therefore support an operator-access comparison. Public replay of either the weekly counts or the contemporaneous mapping requires dated source vintages. Moving to the preceding month's dock map would change the set of contributing docks for 3,499 paired station-hours, even though every dock present in both maps retained its complex assignment. [Source timing, denominators and map comparison](../research/alert_study/may_validation/public_timing/README.md)

### Revisions between forecast issue and the target hour

Among the 1,593 May incidents that contributed paired outcomes, 989 (62.1%) had an archived revision strictly between original issue and the target hour. These incidents contributed 29,998 of 46,621 paired station-hours (64.3%). In 732 incidents the original and latest pre-target messages still had identical affected-route and status fields. In 28 of those incidents, the rule parser changed a station–role assignment at a retained station. An internal text review identified four edits that moved a named segment endpoint. Two retained both endpoints with complete counts and weekly lags. Two other reviewed notices described restored local service in language that the parser treated as a new role. These cases show why a revised-notice experiment needs both timestamped message pairs and review of the operational meaning. The original-issue forecasts use only the first message. [Timing, version pairs and review](../research/alert_study/may_validation/revision_timing/README.md)

## Boston source feasibility

We selected May 14, 2025 for a development check before inspecting Boston notice or demand records. Three non-facility heavy-rail notices named Chinatown or Central and could each be linked to two half-hour faregate records, nearby Bluebikes docks and the same hour one week earlier. All three named the location of an incident. None gave different instructions to passengers at two named stations.

We then screened 95 non-facility first notices created during May whose affected-route records included the Red, Orange or Blue Line. Eleven met the exploratory service-change screen. Seven announced future work, one described replacement service during a switch problem, and three described the same May 22 flood. Among 12 reviewed station-hours from the four immediate notices, ten had both measured outcomes and both weekly lags. All ten came from the flood. The switch-problem endpoints, Braintree and Quincy Adams, lacked assigned docks within 500 m. The three flood notices demonstrate that one disruption can generate distinct IDs and different instructions. Gate state was unknown, limiting interpretation of recorded entries. At 20:44 on May 22 the reported suspension endpoint moved from Jackson Square to Back Bay under the same alert ID. All 22 informed-entity records were identical across this edit, while the numeric severity increased from 7 to 10. A test of revised wording must give both forecast arms that updated severity. Independent disruptions and a held-out period are still needed for forecast replication.
